01 — Fundamentals

Characteristics of Living Things

What makes a living thing different from inert matter? Three general characteristics and three main vital functions.

Made of organic matter

Living things are made of organic matter (unlike inert matter), but they also contain inorganic matter.

Three vital functions

All living things carry out three basic vital functions: they feed, they interact and they reproduce.

Made up of cells

The cell is the basic form of life. Some organisms are single-celled; others are multicellular.

🌿 Organic Substances

Substances unique to living things. Based on carbon networks.

Carbohydrates Lipids Proteins Nucleic acids (DNA)

Carbohydrates: Also called sugars. Glucose is one of them and provides vital energy.

Lipids: Fats and cholesterol. They store energy and are a vital part of the cell membrane.

Proteins: They take part in many processes, mainly in structural functions.

Nucleic acids: DNA is the best known. They contain the genetic information needed for inheritance.

💧 Inorganic Substances

Substances that do not contain carbon skeletons (or only in tiny amounts).

Water Mineral salts

Water: It is the most abundant substance in living things. All reactions take place here.

Mineral salts: They can dissolve in internal fluids, and form solid structures such as bones and shells.

03 — Vital Functions

Nutrition, Interaction and Reproduction

The three main vital functions of living things. Click on each tab to find out more.

Nutrition Function

It is the set of processes through which living things obtain matter and energy needed to carry out the rest of the vital functions.

🌿 Autotrophic Nutrition

Starting from inorganic substances in their environment (water, mineral salts, carbon dioxide), they are able to make their own organic matter. Plants, algae and some bacteria.

🍖 Heterotrophic Nutrition

They need organic matter that is already made, so they feed on other living things or on their remains. Animals, fungi and many microorganisms.

Interaction Function

Living things interact with one another. Animals form relationships of cooperation or competition with each other.

This function also includes the processes by which living things interact with their surroundings: they detect changes in the environment and react to them.

🦎 Adaptation

Even when external conditions change, living things can keep their internal conditions stable thanks to this function. For example, cacti store water and bats hibernate in winter.

Reproduction Function

Through reproduction, living things produce offspring. This process can involve one organism or more than one.

🔄 Asexual Reproduction

Only one organism is involved. The offspring are identical copies of the parent organism. Example: bacteria.

💕 Sexual Reproduction

Two organisms take part, a male and a female. Each one contributes a reproductive cell called a gamete. Both join to form a new individual with traits from both parents.

04 — Cells

The Cell: Basis of Life

All living things, including us, are made up of one or more cells. Cells are the smallest living units.

Structures common to all cells

Structure Function
Cell membrane A very thin layer that surrounds the whole cell and is made of lipids. It controls the passage of substances in and out.
Cytoplasm The inner contents of the cell, made of a viscous fluid and many structures (organelles) that carry out different functions.
Genetic material Its main component is DNA. It contains all the information the cell needs to regulate its cellular processes.

Types of cell — Click to explore

🦠 Prokaryotic Cell

They are cells with the most primitive organisation. They are usually smaller than eukaryotic cells. They belong to single-celled organisms, such as bacteria.

Cell wall

A rigid covering outside the cell membrane. It protects the cell and gives it its shape.

Ribosomes

The only organelle present. They take part in protein synthesis.

Genetic material

It is spread out and free in the cytoplasm — it is not enclosed in a nucleus.

Flagellum

Long extensions that some bacteria have to move and propel themselves.

🔴 Animal Cell (Eukaryotic)

They have a much more complex internal organisation. As they do not have a rigid cell wall, their shape is usually irregular.

Nucleus

It encloses most of the cell's genetic material. It is found towards the centre.

Mitochondria

Organelles that specialise in producing energy for the cell.

Endoplasmic reticulum

A system of channels and membranes responsible for transporting substances.

Golgi apparatus

Organelle that packages, modifies and distributes substances inside and outside the cell.

Centrosome and centrioles

Key structures that take part in and start cell division.

🟢 Plant Cell (Eukaryotic)

They have a thick cell wall that gives them a regular polygonal shape. They have chloroplasts to carry out photosynthesis.

Cell wall

A thick, rigid covering typical of plants. It protects the cell and gives it a regular geometric shape.

Chloroplasts

Organelles that carry out photosynthesis to make their own organic matter. They are green.

Central vacuole

A huge sac that stores water and other substances. It pushes the other organelles to one side.

Nucleus

Because the large vacuole pushes the organelles aside, the nucleus is shifted to one side.

Unable to move

Unlike animal cells, plant cells lack moving structures such as cilia or flagella.

⚖️ Prokaryotes vs Eukaryotes

Feature Prokaryote Eukaryote
Nucleus They have no nucleus — the genetic material is spread through the cytoplasm Yes, they have a defined nucleus surrounded by a membrane
Size Smaller (1-10 μm) Larger (10-100 μm)
Organelles Only ribosomes Many organelles (mitochondria, chloroplasts…)
Examples Bacteria Animals, plants, fungi, protists

Single-celled vs Multicellular

Single-celled Organisms

They are made up of a single cell. This one cell lives independently and carries out all the vital functions of each organism. Examples: bacteria, amoebas.

Multicellular Organisms

They are made up of thousands or millions of cells. Their cells specialise to carry out specific functions and depend on one another to survive. Examples: plants and animals.

Levels of Cell Organisation

Tissues

A group of similar cells that carry out the same activity. For example, muscle tissue.

Organs

Structures made up of several tissues that carry out a specific function. For example, a bone.

Organ Systems

Groups of organs that work together to carry out vital functions.

05 — Taxonomy

History of Classification

The system for classifying living things has changed throughout history as science has advanced.

4th century BC

Aristotle — 2 Kingdoms

The Greek philosopher divided living things into two large groups: those with a "vegetative soul" (plants) and those with a "sensitive soul" (animals).

1735

Carl Linnaeus — 2 Kingdoms

This Swedish naturalist formalised Aristotle's idea by officially establishing the kingdoms Animalia and Plantae, with scientific names.

1858-1865

Owen & Haeckel — 3 Kingdoms

Richard Owen proposed the kingdom Protista. Ernst Haeckel was the first to distinguish single-celled organisms from multicellular ones.

1938

Copeland — 4 Kingdoms

Herbert Copeland introduced the key split between prokaryotes and eukaryotes, adding the kingdom Monera (bacteria without a nucleus).

1969

Whittaker — 5 Kingdoms

Robert Whittaker finally separated fungi from plants and animals: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia.

06 — Kingdoms

The Five Kingdoms

Today, all living things are classified into five main kingdoms. Click on each card to see the details.

Animal Kingdom

It includes all vertebrate and invertebrate animals.

Heterotrophic Multicellular Eukaryotic With tissues

Type of nutrition: heterotrophic. Cell organisation: multicellular. Type of cell: eukaryotic. Presence of tissues: with true tissues. Examples: marmot, lizard, bat, octopus, earthworm.

Plant Kingdom

It includes mosses, ferns and plants with seeds or flowers.

Autotrophic Multicellular Eukaryotic With tissues

Type of nutrition: autotrophic. Cell organisation: multicellular. Type of cell: eukaryotic. Presence of tissues: with true tissues. Examples: oak, fern, daisy, moss, pine.

Fungi Kingdom

Yeasts, moulds and the mushrooms we see in the woods.

Heterotrophic Both types Eukaryotic

Type of nutrition: heterotrophic. Cell organisation: single-celled or multicellular. Type of cell: eukaryotic. Presence of tissues: without true tissues. Examples: bread yeast, fruit mould, mushrooms (genus Amanita).

Protist Kingdom

It groups together protozoa and algae.

Autotrophic / Heterotrophic Eukaryotic

Type of nutrition: autotrophic or heterotrophic. Cell organisation: single-celled or multicellular. Type of cell: eukaryotic. Presence of tissues: without true tissues. Examples: paramecium, amoeba, red and green algae.

Monera Kingdom

This kingdom includes bacteria and cyanobacteria.

Autotrophic / Heterotrophic Prokaryotic Single-celled

Type of nutrition: autotrophic or heterotrophic. Cell organisation: mostly single-celled. Type of cell: prokaryotic. Presence of tissues: without true tissues. Examples: spirilla, bacilli (Lactobacillus), cyanobacteria.

⚠️ Threats to Biodiversity

Pollution

Pollution of the air, water and soil damages habitats and seriously affects species.

Habitat loss

Deforestation, extensive livestock farming and extreme urbanisation destroy many habitats.

Climate Change

Rising temperatures alter ecosystems, and many species struggle to adapt.

Invasive species

Species brought from elsewhere compete with local species or prey on them, and seriously threaten local biodiversity.

Overexploitation

Excessive hunting, overfishing and uncontrolled logging cause the decline and disappearance of many species.

Types of species: Native, Non-native and Invasive

Native Species

It is the species that originally lives in a particular place. It has developed in the area and lives in complete harmony with its ecosystem. For example, the oak in the Basque Country.

Non-native Species

It is a species that has been brought from another place and would not naturally live in that area. Humans often introduce it, either on purpose or by accident.

Invasive Species

It is a non-native (exotic) species that harms native species, as it competes for the same habitat and indirectly attacks the whole ecosystem.

08 — Threat

Invasive Species in the Basque Country

In our region there are several invasive species that cause serious damage to local biodiversity.

Asian Hornet

Vespa velutina

It arrived from Asia. It attacks our native bees, destroying whole hives. By reducing the bee population, which is essential for pollination, it seriously endangers farming and local plant ecosystems.

⚠️ High danger

Pampas grass

Cortaderia selloana

An invasive plant from South America. It grows incredibly fast, taking over huge open areas (roadsides, fields), completely wiping out native vegetation and stopping it from growing.

⚠️ Rapid spread

Red-eared slider

Trachemys scripta

A turtle sold as a pet and then irresponsibly released into rivers and lakes. It fiercely competes with the native European pond turtle (Emys orbicularis), taking away both its vital food and its sunny basking spots.

⚠️ Unfair competition

Japanese knotweed

Reynoutria japonica

A plant from East Asia. It is almost impossible to eradicate because it has a deep root system. It replaces the natural plant cover on riverbanks, causing serious erosion and soil degradation.

⚠️ Almost impossible to eradicate
